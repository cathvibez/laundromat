## 1. Manufacturing

### 1.1 The three routes, with real numbers

"buy it if you really want it" long tail. It is **not** a viable retail product — at $25 cost
you'd need a $50+ MSRP to have any margin, and nobody buys a $50 unknown small-box card game.

#### Full offset at scale (5,000+)

Not relevant to you yet. For reference, the industry rule of thumb quoted repeatedly is ~$8/unit
at 1,000, ~$6 at 3,000, ~$5 at 5,000 for a *mid-complexity* game
([LaunchBoom, 2026](https://www.launchboom.com/game-tips/cost-to-publish-a-board-game/) —
403s to automated fetch but is indexed and widely quoted). Your game is well below mid-complexity,
hence the lower numbers above.

### 1.2 Where the cost cliff actually is

There are two cliffs, and only one of them is about volume:

1. **POD → offset, at 500 units.** Unit cost falls **~5×** (≈$22 → ≈$4.50). This is by far the
   biggest step in the whole exercise. 500 is the MOQ that unlocks it (PrintNinja, LongPack,
   Whatz). Total cash outlay at 500 units ≈ **$2,200–2,800 ex-works** — surprisingly small.
2. **500 → 1,000, roughly −30%; 1,000 → 3,000, roughly another −30%.** Real but not
   transformative. Past 3,000 the curve flattens hard.

The cliff that matters more for a first print run is **not** volume — it is **component
class**. Moving from "cards + one punchboard sheet + stock die" to "cards + 7 separate rigid
tiles + a custom plastic figure" roughly **doubles** unit cost *and* raises your MOQ from 500
to 1,500–2,000, i.e. it multiplies your capital requirement by ~6.

### 1.3 Shipping, freight and the tariff problem ⚠️

This is the section most likely to be stale, and the most likely to kill your numbers.

- **Sea freight, China→US, 40ft container, 2026:** roughly **$3,400–$5,000** (West Coast) to
  **$4,100–$5,000** (East Coast) as of March 2026, rising to **$6,500–$9,800** by July 2026 as
  peak season began ([gortofreight rate tracker](https://gortofreight.com/container-rates-from-china-to-us/),
  July 2026). A 40ft container holds on the order of 8,000–12,000 small-box games, so freight
  is **$0.40–$1.00/unit at container scale**. At 500–1,000 units you ship LCL or air and freight
  is **$1.50–$4.00/unit** — often comparable to the manufacturing cost itself.
- **Stonemaier's own worked example** budgets **$3.00/unit freight** on a $6.50 manufacturing
  cost for a Tuscany-sized box
  ([A Price Formula for Your Product](https://stonemaiergames.com/a-price-formula-your-product-for-direct-and-retail/)).
- **US tariffs on Chinese-made games.** Tabletop games import under HS 9504.90.6000. Rates have
  been wildly volatile: 20% (Mar 2025) → 54% → 145% (Apr 2025) → 30% under a 90-day truce
  (May 2025) → threatened 130% (Oct 2025). Publishers spent late 2025 planning on **30%**
  continuing into 2026
  ([BoardGameWire, Oct 2025](https://boardgamewire.com/index.php/2025/10/17/production-will-stop-shipments-will-be-frozen-and-some-companies-may-not-make-it-board-game-industry-braces-for-further-pain-after-trump-threatens-130-tariffs-on-china/)).
  As of **July 2026** the real-world effect is documented: Czech Games' CEO reported tariffs
  drove a **$30 price gap between US and European editions** of the same game
  ([BoardGameWire, 3 Jul 2026](https://boardgamewire.com/index.php/2026/07/03/czech-games-ceo-says-us-tariffs-drove-30-price-gap-between-us-european-editions-of-kingdom-come-deliverance-board-game/)).
  Formal customs entry is now required on every shipment regardless of value, adding
  **$125–300 in broker fees per shipment**.
  Stonemaier's [Math of Tariffs](https://stonemaiergames.com/the-math-of-tariffs/) (Apr 2025)
  models the honest middle path: $10 production → $15 with tariff → MSRP $50 → $60.
- **Practical implication for you.** **Get a current landed-cost quote, not a manufacturing
  quote.** Ask every vendor for ex-works + freight + duty + broker, delivered to your door. A
  $4.50 unit cost can become $7.50 landed in the US.
- **If you are UK/EU-based**, this problem largely evaporates for your home market, and a UK
  vendor like **Ivory Graphics** at MOQ 50–100 becomes a genuinely attractive first-run option
  that no US-based designer has.

### 1.4 Component decisions ranked by cost impact

1. **The raccoon.** Custom plastic mini or moulded wooden meeple: **+$0.35–0.90/unit +
   $1,500–5,000 tooling + MOQ ×2–4**. A **printed cardboard standee with a plastic clip** costs
   **~$0.08–0.15/unit** and shares your existing punchboard sheet. **Recommendation: standee for
   the first print run.** Jimothy loses nothing thematically; you can promise a deluxe meeple as
   a stretch goal later, when someone else's money is paying for the mould.
2. **The 7 machine tiles.** If they are 7 individually die-cut rigid boards you are paying setup
   per die and per board. If they are **7 punched pieces on one 2mm greyboard punchboard sheet**
   (which also carries the 7 on/off markers, the key token and the Jimothy standee) the whole
   sheet costs **~$0.35–0.70/unit**. **Recommendation: one punchboard sheet.** Second-best:
   **make the machines large cards** (~$0.05 each) and put the on/off state on a flip. Test
   whether flipped cards read as well as tiles at the table — if they do, you have removed a
   whole component class.
3. **The box.** A two-piece rigid telescoping box is standard for retail and adds roughly
   **$0.70–1.40/unit** over a tuck box. For a 108-card + punchboard game you want a **small
   two-piece box** (roughly 5.5″×5.5″×2″ or a 7.5″×5.5″ "small box"). A tuck box saves money but
   costs you retail shelf credibility and cannot hold a punchboard well. **Keep the two-piece
   box.** This is the one place not to save.
4. **Card count and size.** 108 cards at 63×88mm is a sweet spot: it is a standard sheet-efficient
   count, it fits standard tuck/tray tooling, and it is the exact capacity of common POD boxes
   (TGC sells a "Poker Tuck Box (108 cards)"). **If your special-item count lands you at 109–120
   cards you may cross into a second printing sheet.** Ask your printer where the sheet break is
   and design the special-item deck to land just under it. Concretely: 84 + 4 = 88 fixed, so you
   have **20 special-item cards free** before you hit 108. Budget exactly 20.
5. **Card stock and finish.** 300–310gsm blue-core is the retail standard. **Linen finish costs
   essentially nothing extra at offset** (it is an embossing roller, not a material change) and
   is what players associate with quality; smooth prints marginally crisper art. Linen also hides
   handling wear. **Recommendation: 300gsm blue core, linen.** Blue/black core matters here
   because your 84 item cards are color-coded by owner — you do not want backlit cards revealing
   shade through the back. Verify opacity with a physical sample.
6. **The die.** Stock d6, **$0.03–0.10**. Custom-pipped or custom-faced d6 (which you may want,
   given your die maps to actions): **$0.15–0.35** plus possible tooling. A **custom-faced d6 is
   cheap enough to be worth it** — and per your own comparables notes, printing the action on the
   face fixes a real teaching problem.
7. **Shrinkwrap and insert.** Shrinkwrap ~$0.05–0.10 and non-negotiable for retail. A vac-formed
   plastic insert is **not** worth it at this scale (and has its own 1,000 MOQ at PrintNinja).
   Use a cardboard divider.

**Modeled bill of materials at 1,000 units, recommended spec:**

| Item | Unit cost |
|---|---|
| 108 cards, 300gsm blue core linen, 63×88 | $1.55 |
| 1 punchboard sheet, 2mm greyboard, 2-sided | $0.50 |
| Rulebook, 8pp, A5, full color | $0.25 |
| Custom d6 | $0.20 |
| Two-piece box + divider | $1.00 |
| Shrinkwrap + assembly | $0.15 |
| **Ex-works total** | **≈ $3.65** |
| + freight & duty (US, 30% ⚠️) | **≈ $5.80–6.50 landed** |
| + freight (UK/EU, domestic or EU print) | **≈ $4.20–4.80 landed** |

At 5× landed that is a **$29–33 MSRP in the US, $21–24 in the UK/EU**. That is a defensible
price for a 108-card box. Note the tariff alone costs you a price tier.

## 2. Prototype → product: what you must produce before anyone quotes

### 2.1 What a manufacturer literally requires

Nobody quotes off a description. To get a *binding* quote you need a **spec sheet** (component
list with exact dimensions, counts, materials, finishes, box size). To get *files accepted* you
need:

- **Vector or 300 DPI raster art, CMYK, at final size + bleed.** PrintNinja requires **0.125″
  (3.175mm) bleed on all four sides** and a **0.125″ safe margin** inside the trim, and their
  prepress team checks "bleed, resolution, color mode, and die-line accuracy" on every file
  ([PrintNinja game board setup guides](https://printninja.com/printing-resource-center/printninja-file-setup-checklist/custom-game-guidelines/game-board-setup-guides/)).
  European vendors typically ask 3mm. Design to 3.175mm and you satisfy both.
- **CMYK, not RGB.** Everything you design on screen is RGB. Saturated RGB blues, oranges and
  greens do not exist in CMYK and will print muddy. **This matters enormously for this game
  specifically**, because your entire mechanic is 6 player colors × 2 shades = 12 distinguishable
  colors that must be distinguishable *in print, under bad lighting, by colorblind players.*
  Get a **physical proof** before committing. Budget for a colorblind-safe palette plus a
  redundant non-color cue (icon, shade pattern, or a printed dark/light glyph) — this is a
  design requirement, not an accessibility nicety, given tiers 3 and 4 of your reckoning.
- **Dielines** supplied by the manufacturer for box, punchboard and any non-rectangular pieces.
  You do not draw these; you request them and design onto the layer they give you. Panda publishes
  a [Design Guidebook](https://pandagm.com/tool_file/design-guidebook/); PrintNinja publishes
  per-component [setup guides](https://printninja.com/custom-board-games/custom-board-game-file-setup/).
- **A rulebook** as a print-ready PDF with page count divisible by 4.
- **Fonts outlined, images embedded, PDF/X-1a or press-ready PDF.**

Ivory Graphics will check print-ready PDFs **free**, and bill fixes at **£60–100/hr** — a useful
sanity gate ([Ivory](https://www.ivory.co.uk/custom-card-game-printing)).

### 2.2 Art — what it actually costs

Published rates, all retrieved Aug 2026:

- **Per illustration:** $150–$350 is the commonly quoted band from professional board game
  illustrators; fully rendered detailed card art runs **$250 to $1,000+**. Real published
  examples: **$75 per small loot card, $350 per character, $600 per large frame illustration**.
- **Hourly:** professional illustrators from **$50/hr** minimum, commonly **$50–100/hr**.
- **Typical whole-game budget:** the most-cited figure is **~$3,000 for 35–40 illustrations**
  (~$75–85 each), with **$3,000–$4,000** described as a typical indie artwork budget.
  ([Pine Island Games](https://www.pineislandgames.com/blog/illustration-a-comprehensive-guide),
  [Stonemaier, A Guide to Board Game Illustration](https://stonemaiergames.com/a-guide-to-board-game-illustration/),
  [LaunchBoom](https://www.launchboom.com/game-tips/how-to-find-and-hire-an-artist-for-your-board-game/))
- **Exclusivity surcharge:** ~**+50%** if the artist may not resell or reuse the work.

**How many illustrations does Laundromat actually need?** Fewer than you'd think, and this is a
real advantage:

- 7 garment types × 2 shades = **14 base garment illustrations**, recolored 6× via a flat color
  layer. You are not paying for 84 illustrations; you are paying for 14 pieces built as
  **recolorable line art with a separated flat**. Say this in the brief. **~14 × $150 = $2,100.**
- 7 special items + 4 events = **11 illustrations**, of which 2 (Jimothy, the Gang) want more
  care. **~$1,800–2,500.**
- Box cover: **$600–1,500.** This is the single highest-ROI art spend you will make and should
  not be the cheapest thing you buy.
- Machine tile art: **1 illustration, reused 7×. ~$250.**
- **Realistic total illustration budget: $4,500–$7,000.** You can get to ~$2,500 with a stylized
  flat-vector approach and a cheaper artist, and to ~$1,200 with a competent stock/asset-marketplace
  build (see below), at real cost to shelf appeal.

**Where to find illustrators:** ArtStation (browse, then DM — best quality signal),
Dribbble's [board game illustration service listings](https://dribbble.com/services/32540-Board-Game-Illustration-Design),
the **Board Game Designers Forum** and **Board Game Design Lab** Facebook group job boards,
Cardboard Edison's newsletter, and — for a first game — the artist credits of small-box games
whose look you envy (they list the illustrator on BGG; many are freelancers taking work).
Fiverr/Upwork exist and are cheap; the quality variance is enormous and the licensing terms are
often wrong for commercial print. If you use them, **get commercial print rights in writing,
plus source files.**

**Asset marketplaces** (Adobe Stock, Creative Market, Game-Icons.net, Kenney) are viable for
*icons and UI*, near-useless for a cover, and a genuine legal hazard if you don't check the
license permits commercial physical reproduction. Game-Icons.net (CC-BY) is the standard free
source for the ~15 iconography marks this game needs (dark/light, clothes/linen, on/off,
crowding, wash-count).

### 2.3 Graphic design ≠ illustration — and it is the bigger risk here

This is the part first-time designers underestimate, and for *this specific game* it is the
difference between shipping and failing. Illustration is "what does a blanket look like."
Graphic design is:

- the **card layout and information hierarchy** — 108 cards where the player must instantly read
  owner-color, shade, garment type, and category (clothes vs linen);
- the **icon language** for the reckoning tiers, crowding, the underwear/blanket exceptions, and
  the socks-need-a-second-wash rule;
- the **machine tile layout** showing capacity 4, on/off state and Jimothy occupancy;
- the **player aid**, which for a game with a 4-tier precedence ladder plus three filters is
  **mandatory, not optional**;
- **rulebook layout and typesetting**;
- **box face design**, spine, back-of-box (the 30-second sales pitch);
- the **colorblind-safe 12-color system**.

Published rates: **$40/hr for less established designers to ~$100/hr for veterans**; whole-game
board game graphic design commonly quoted at **$4,000–$15,000+**, with simple projects from
**$500–5,000** ([Pine Island](https://www.pineislandgames.com/blog/illustration-a-comprehensive-guide),
[Jimms Design](https://blog.jimmsdesign.co.uk/how-much-does-it-cost-to-design-a-board-game-non-manufacture/)).

**For Laundromat, budget $3,000–6,000 for graphic design** and treat it as at least as important
as illustration. Some illustrators do both; most do not, and you should ask directly.

**Rulebook.** Hire a **rules editor** separately. This game has a precedence ladder, three
exception filters, a two-deck timing rule ("cards cannot be played the day they are drawn"), a
key that passes at a specific moment, and a simultaneous-victory clause. A rules editor costs
**$300–900** for a game this size and will save you a hundred BGG rules threads. Cardboard Edison
and the BGDL community both maintain editor lists.

**Total pre-manufacturing spend, realistic: $8,000–14,000.** You can compress to ~$4,000 with a
single stylized artist doing double duty and you doing layout yourself in Affinity Publisher /
InDesign, but only if you already have design chops.

## 3. Self-publish vs license

### 3.1 Licensing: how it actually works

**The money.** Royalties are **5–8% of wholesale for a newer designer**, rising for designers with
hits. Wholesale is **~40% of MSRP** (distributors buy at a 60% discount off MSRP; direct-to-retail
is a 45–50% discount)
([Pine Island on licensing contracts](https://www.pineislandgames.com/blog/breaking-down-a-game-licensing-contract),
[Board Game Design Course](https://boardgamedesigncourse.com/how-designers-get-paid/),
[Stonemaier price formula](https://stonemaiergames.com/a-price-formula-your-product-for-direct-and-retail/)).

**Worked example for Laundromat.** MSRP $30 → wholesale ~$12 → 6% royalty = **$0.72/copy.**

| Copies sold | Your gross royalty |
|---|---|
| 2,000 (typical first print run for a small publisher) | **$1,440** |
| 5,000 | **$3,600** |
| 10,000 (a genuine success) | **$7,200** |
| 50,000 (a hit) | **$36,000** |

**Advances** for newer designers: **$500–$2,000**, and typically **$700** in the commonly cited
example — and an advance is *against* royalties, not on top. Escalators are common: e.g. 5% on
the first 5,000, 6–7% above that. Payment is quarterly or semi-annual, ~30 days after period end.

**That is the honest number, and it is small.** Almost no first-time designer earns meaningful
money from one licensed game. What licensing buys you is: **zero capital risk, zero warehousing,
zero fulfillment, professional art and graphic design paid for by someone else, distribution
access you cannot buy, and a credential for your second game.**

**How pitching works.**

1. **Find publishers who take unsolicited submissions.** The two live resources:
   - **[Cardboard Edison Compendium](https://cardboardedison.com/directoryinfo/)** — 350+ publisher
     profiles, whether they're open to submissions, what they publish, where they're based,
     which cons they attend, how they want to be contacted. **Last updated July 2026.** ~$5/mo
     Patreon. **Buy this. It is the single highest-value $5 in this whole document.**
   - **[Pam Walls' monthly "Publishers accepting board game submissions" list](https://pamwallsgamedesign.substack.com/p/publishers-accepting-board-game-submissions-9bc)**
     — free, updated monthly (June 2026 edition live).
2. **Send a sell sheet, not a game.** A sell sheet is a **one-page PDF**: title + logo, a one-line
   hook, player count / age / playtime / weight, 3–5 bullet mechanics, one "why this is different"
   line, 2–3 component photos, your contact and a link to a rules PDF and a how-to-play video.
   The framing publishers actually respond to is **Familiarity + Innovation**: what they'll
   recognize, and what's new
   ([Hero Time sell sheet guide](https://herotime1.com/marketing/board-game-sell-sheets-complete-guide/),
   [Mindclash, from a publisher's perspective](https://mindclashgames.com/how-to-pitch-a-board-game-to-a-publisher-from-a-publishers-perspective/)).
3. **Include a how-to-play video.** Several publishers (e.g. [Rock Manor Games](https://rockmanorgames.com/game-submissions/))
   explicitly give preference to submissions with video. A 4-minute screen-recorded runthrough over
   your prototype is enough. This is free and most designers don't do it.
4. **Pitch in person at conventions** if you can. Publishers take meetings at Gen Con, Essen, UKGE
   and PAX Unplugged, and a 20-minute demo beats fifty emails.

**Realistic timeline, pitch → shelf: 2.5 to 4 years.** 3–12 months of pitching and rejections;
3–9 months of publisher-side development and re-theming; 6–12 months art, production and
manufacture; 2–4 months freight and distribution. Assume you have **no control** over theme,
title, art or even several rules once signed — publishers routinely re-theme.

| Trademark filing (US, 1 class) | $350 + optional $300–800 clearance search |
| Review copies (30 × unit + shipping) | $500 – 900 |
| Crowdfunding platform + processing fees (~8–10% of raise) | see §4 |
| Paid ads / pre-launch marketing | $2,000 – 10,000 |
| **Total, before fulfillment** | **≈ $17,000 – 32,000 (US)** / **≈ $13,000 – 24,000 (UK/EU)** |

Then the ongoing operational reality:

- **Warehousing.** A 1,000-unit run of a small box is roughly 3–4 pallets. Self-warehousing in a
  spare room is genuinely viable at this size and is what most first-timers do. 3PL warehousing
  runs ~$15–40/pallet/month plus receiving.
- **Fulfillment.** Per-order pick/pack **$2.50–5.00** plus postage. Domestic postage on a
  ~600–800g small box: **$5–9 US, £3–5 UK**. International individual shipping is brutal
  ($20–40) — this is the classic campaign-killer. Use regional hubs or don't offer the region.
- **Distribution vs direct.** Distributors buy at **60% off MSRP**, so a $30 game nets you **$12**.
  Direct sales net you ~$30 minus payment processing and shipping — but you have to find every
  buyer yourself. Distribution is how you reach the other 99%, and it is why the 5× rule exists.
- **The margin structure and why 5×.** Industry norm is **MSRP = 5–7× manufacturing cost**;
  Stonemaier starts at **manufacturing × 5** and adjusts for freight and royalties, and admits
  some of their own games sit at only 4× with uncomfortably tight margins
  ([Stonemaier](https://stonemaiergames.com/a-price-formula-your-product-for-direct-and-retail/)).
  The arithmetic: at 5×, a $30 MSRP game costs $6 landed, nets $12 from a distributor, leaves $6
  gross per unit — out of which come art amortization, warehousing, damages, returns, marketing,
  and your time. **If your MSRP is under 5× landed cost, you do not have a business, you have a
  subsidy.** Note that under US tariffs 5× may not be achievable at a price consumers accept —
  which is exactly why publishers are shrinking their 2026 release slates.

### 3.3 The tie-breaker

Self-publishing is a **business**, not a publishing decision. It requires you to be a marketer, a
logistics operator, a customer service desk and a small importer for 18–24 months. The frequently
cited failure pattern is real: **one-game publishers rarely survive**, because you're competing
with companies that have catalogues, mailing lists and repeat buyers, and your fixed costs are
amortized across exactly one SKU
([BoardGameBiz](https://www.boardgamebiz.com/post/why-80-of-board-game-startups-fail-and-how-to-be-in-the-20-that-succeed)).

### 3.4 Which route suits *you*

**License it. Clearly.** Reasons specific to this game and this designer:

1. **No audience.** Crowdfunding math (§4) says you need ~2,000+ engaged emails to fund a small
   campaign. You have zero. Building that list is a 6–12 month, $3,000–10,000 project *before* you
   spend a cent on manufacturing.
2. **The prior-art problem is a marketing problem (§7), and marketing is precisely what a publisher
   is good at and you are not.** A publisher will also cheerfully re-theme the game out of the
   collision if that's the right call — which is a solution available to them and painful for you.
3. **The commercial profile is awkward for a self-publisher.** 3–6 players, 45–135 minutes,
   open-information tactical, moderate-heavy rules, cozy-mundane theme. That is neither a
   20-minute filler (cheap to sell) nor a heavy euro (a passionate niche). Publishers know how to
   place that; a first-timer selling direct usually cannot.
4. **The variance is asymmetric.** Licensing: worst case you lose 6–18 months of pitching and a
   few hundred dollars, and you learn how publishers think. Self-publishing: worst case you lose
   $20,000+ and sit on 800 unsold copies.
5. **Your own comparables doc lists five significant open design risks** (kingmaking endgame,
   modal-null reckoning, output randomness, key fairness, tier-2 inversion). A publisher's
   developer will fix these with a budget. Self-publishing means shipping them.

**One legitimate middle path:** self-publish a **small, cheap POD/short-run edition (250–500
copies)** to build a track record and BGG presence, explicitly framed as a limited first edition,
while pitching. But do it only if the money is money you can afford to lose.

### 4.1 Kickstarter vs Gamefound, August 2026

| | Kickstarter | Gamefound |
|---|---|---|
| 2024 tabletop $ raised | **$220M** | **$156M** |
| Avg tabletop campaign | **$41,400** | **$395,000** |
| Top-10 tabletop campaigns 2024 | 4 | **6** |
| Fees | 5% platform + 3–5% + $0.20–0.30 processing ≈ **8–10% all-in** ([KS fees](https://www.kickstarter.com/help/fees)) | ~5% + processing; built-in pledge manager (KS charges separately for one) |
| Discovery for unknowns | Better — bigger casual audience, "Micro Game May" and other open calls | Weaker — audience is enthusiast/deluxe-oriented |
| Geography | Stronger NA/AU/UK | Stronger EU |

([BoardGameWire, Feb 2025](https://boardgamewire.com/index.php/2025/02/06/our-ambition-for-2025-is-to-be-number-one-in-tabletop-gamefound-closes-gap-on-kickstarter-again-as-crowdfunding-giants-2024-dollars-raised-remains-flat/);
[LaunchBoom platform comparison, 2026](https://www.launchboom.com/crowdfunding-guides/kickstarter-vs-gamefound-vs-backerkit/))

**Reading the numbers honestly:** Gamefound is winning *the top of the market* — big-box, deluxe,
miniatures-heavy, publisher-run campaigns from creators who bring their own audience. That $395k
average is not a sign that Gamefound is easier; it is a sign that only established projects go
there. **Kickstarter is still the right platform for an unknown small-box game**, because (a) it
has the larger drive-by audience, (b) its 2025–26 "Micro Game May" open call specifically supports
small games and first-time creators, and (c) tabletop had an **80% success rate on Kickstarter in
2024 — 5,314 of 6,646 campaigns funded**, its highest ever.

### 4.2 A realistic funding target

For a 108-card small-box game with no audience:

- **Set the public goal at the true minimum you can produce for.** For 1,000 units at ~$3.65 plus
  freight/duty, plus platform fees (~9%) and fulfillment reserve, the honest floor is
  **$18,000–25,000** — but the *public* goal should be set lower, at the point where you'd still
  ship, typically **$8,000–15,000**. Publicly low goals fund faster, and funding velocity is the
  algorithm.
- **The Botany case study is the clearest published worked example**: public goal **$5,000**,
  internal success threshold **$22,000**, unit cost **$8**, MOQ **1,500**, production threshold
  **$12,000**, total-with-advertising threshold **$22,000**. It raised **$1,057,307 from 15,105
  backers** — but that was with an agency-run pre-campaign that had already banked **2,600 $1
  reservations** ([Board Game Design Course case study](https://boardgamedesigncourse.com/calculating-what-you-need-to-get-your-game-funded-a-case-study/)).
- **A realistic un-agencied first campaign for a small-box game is $10,000–40,000 and 300–1,200
  backers.** Anything above that requires either an existing audience or real ad spend.

### 4.3 Conversion rates — the numbers that decide everything

| Channel | Conversion to backer |
|---|---|
| Plain email list (cold-ish signups) | **1–5%** |
| Opted-in "notify me at launch" emails | **5–15%** |
| **Kickstarter "Follow" button** | **~30% average and median** (some sources say 8–11%) |
| **$1 / small paid reservation** | **35–48%** (Botany hit 48%) |

([Prelaunch Club](https://prelaunch.marketing/blogs/academy/average-conversion-rates-for-kickstarter-followers),
[PledgeBox](https://www.pledgebox.com/post/what-you-need-to-know-to-go-from-follower-to-backer),
[Board Game Design Course](https://boardgamedesigncourse.com/calculating-what-you-need-to-get-your-game-funded-a-case-study/))

**The list size you need.** At a $35 average pledge and a $25,000 target you need ~700 backers.
Working backwards:

- via email at 5%: **14,000 emails** — unrealistic
- via KS followers at 30%: **~2,400 followers** — realistic and the number to chase
- mixed realistic build: **2,000–3,000 email signups + 1,500–2,500 KS followers**

The widely repeated benchmarks — "at least 2,000 emails at 5%" and "aim for 10,000 followers"
([Meeple Marketing](https://meeplemarketing.com/how-to-build-an-email-list-for-your-board-game-launch/),
[Neutronium, 2026](https://neutronium.games/blog/kickstarter-board-game-tips)) — bracket the same
truth: **the 30 days before launch matter more than the 30 days of the campaign, and campaigns
that hit 30% of goal in the first 48 hours almost always fund while those that don't almost never
recover.**

### 4.4 Required campaign assets

Non-negotiable, in rough order of impact:

1. **Finished or near-finished art.** Campaigns with placeholder art do not fund. This is why
   crowdfunding does **not** de-risk the art spend — you pay for art *before* you raise money.
2. **A how-to-play video** (3–6 min) and a **short hype video** (60–90s).
3. **A full playthrough** by a known creator, or at minimum a clean overhead playthrough of your own.
4. **A public, downloadable rulebook PDF** — sophisticated backers read it and its absence reads
   as "not finished."
5. **A Tabletop Simulator or Screentop.gg build** so anyone can try it. Free, and converts.
6. **3–8 professional review videos/articles live in week 1.**
7. **Photographed physical components** (this is what the pre-production proof is for).
8. **Honest, itemized shipping costs by region, quoted in writing before launch.**

### 4.5 How first campaigns fail — ranked

1. **No pre-launch audience.** Overwhelmingly the top cause.
2. **Shipping/fulfillment math wrong.** "Setting reward prices before knowing fulfillment costs" —
   the canonical funded-but-failed pattern is a spreadsheet off by a third or a shipping quote
   nobody got in writing ([WinSBS, Jan 2026](https://blog.winsbs.com/2026/01/28/kickstarter-board-game-fulfillment-risk-why-projects-run-late-or-fail/)).
3. **Weak visuals.** Backers judge the box image in 1.5 seconds.
4. **No marketing spend and no creator coverage.** For scale: Space Chef spent **$20,000+ on
   marketing, ads and creators to raise $50,000 from 1,119 backers**.
5. **No solo mode.** A growing structural disadvantage — and Laundromat, being 3–6 players with
   heavy interaction, has no plausible solo mode. This is a real, unfixable campaign handicap.
6. **Goal set too high**, so the campaign never gets algorithmic momentum.

([Stonemaier, Top 10 Reasons Your Kickstarter May Fail](https://stonemaiergames.com/top-10-reasons-your-kickstarter-campaign-may-fail/))

### 4.6 Comparable outcomes ⚠️

Honest caveat: aggregated small-box campaign data is thin and the headline campaigns are
unrepresentative. What I can source:

- **Micro Game May (Kickstarter open call): 84% of micro-game projects meet their funding goal**,
  and Kickstarter ran six open calls in 2025 supporting **~700 first-time creators**
  ([Kickstarter, Micro May 2026](https://updates.kickstarter.com/micro-may-2026-small-games-big-ideas/)).
- **Space Chef: $50,000 from 1,119 backers**, on $20,000+ marketing spend.
- **Double Combo Games (French indie card game, 2026): funded in 48 minutes, ~$85,000**
  ([PRUnderground](https://www.prunderground.com/card-game-from-double-combo-games-a-french-indie-studio-funds-on-kickstarter-in-just-48-minutes-approaching-85000/00343605/)).
- Top-of-market for context and *not* a comparable: Cyberpunk TCG, **$26.95M / 47,676 backers**,
  March 2026.

**Sanity number to plan against: 400–900 backers, $15,000–35,000, if you do the pre-launch work
properly. Under 150 backers and under $6,000 if you don't.**

## 5. Marketing and community

### 5.1 BoardGameGeek

BGG is not optional; it is where the hobby's memory lives.

- **Get the game into the database.** Submit the **publisher entry and designer (person) entry
  first**, then the game — there is a known chicken-and-egg annoyance where a game needs a
  publisher and a publisher entry benefits from a game
  ([BGG wiki: How To Add a BGG Publisher](https://boardgamegeek.com/wiki/page/How_To_Add_a_BGG_Publisher),
  [BGG News: Advice for Designers and Publishers](https://boardgamegeek.com/blogpost/36873/advice-designers-and-publishers-how-submit-listing)).
  Approval takes days to weeks. **Do this the moment you have a name, a cover image and a firm
  release plan — not later.**
- Fill in **primary name, a real two-paragraph description, player count, playtime, weight,
  mechanics tags, designer, artist, publisher**. Upload good images. Upload the rulebook PDF as a
  file. These are all free and all rank.
- **Build presence before the game exists**: post design-diary blog entries on your BGG blog, be
  a useful participant in the *Board Game Design* and *Works in Progress* forums, log the games
  you play. BGG is unforgiving of accounts that appear only to promote.
- **The BGG "Hotness" and the "Crowdfunding" forums** are meaningful traffic sources during a
  campaign. Getting geeklisted matters.

### 5.2 Reviewers and content creators

**Cost reality.** You do not pay for reviews. Reputable publishers "do not pay reviewers to
express their opinions, nor charge reviewers for the products they review"
([Stonemaier, 2026 reviewer policy](https://stonemaiergames.com/our-current-approach-to-board-game-reviewers-and-content-creators-2026/)).
You pay for **copies and postage**.

Stonemaier's actual operating numbers, as a scale reference: **~1,000 free products per year**;
**10–12 advance review copies** by airmail months ahead; **~200 reviewers contacted per launch, of
whom 50–75 accept**; and they redirect **damaged customer returns** to reviewers rather than
binning them.

**Your version of that, sized for a first game:**

| Line | Cost |
|---|---|
| 25–35 review copies at ~$4–6 unit | $120 – 200 |
| Postage (mix of domestic + international, $6–25 each) | $400 – 800 |
| Customs reimbursement reserve | $100 |
| **Total** | **≈ $600 – 1,100** |

If you're pre-manufacturing, review copies must be **POD prototypes at $20–28 each**, which turns
30 copies into **$700–1,000 in units alone**. Budget for this; it surprises people.

**Who covers small-box games** (approach in roughly this order — smaller creators respond, larger
ones need a track record):

- **Written/blog:** Meeple Mountain, What's Eric Playing?, The Thoughtful Gamer, Roll to Review,
  Board Game Quest, Zatu blog, The Family Gamers.
- **Video/YouTube:** No Pun Included, Shut Up & Sit Down (aspirational, rarely covers unknowns),
  Actualol (specifically loves small/cheap/clever games), Man vs Meeple, Before You Play, The
  Broken Meeple, Rahdo (runthroughs), Gaming Rules! (rules videos), Watch It Played (rules
  videos — often paid work, and worth it).
- **Small-box specialists and micro-game reviewers** are your real target: they have engaged
  audiences, fast turnaround, and low barriers.
- **BGG reviewers** with high-visibility blogs.

**How to approach:** read their submission page first (most have one, and most say what they will
and won't cover); email a **short** pitch with the sell sheet, a rules PDF link, a playthrough
video link and a Screentop link; state the release/campaign date and offer a copy; **never** ask
for a positive review, and disclose nothing beyond "you'll receive a free copy." Follow up once.
Lead time: contact **8–12 weeks** before you need coverage live.

## 6. Naming and trademark: "Laundromat"

### 6.1 The legal position

**In the US, "laundromat" is a genericized former trademark.** Westinghouse coined it in 1940
(credited to George Edward Pendray) for a wall-mountable automatic washing machine; the last
Westinghouse registration is recorded around **1952**, after which the term passed into generic
use for any self-service laundry
([Jones IP Law, "Genericide: The Case of Laundromat"](https://www.jonesipl.com/article/genericide-the-case-of-laundromat/);
[Consumer Reports on genericization](https://www.consumerreports.org/consumerist/15-product-trademarks-that-have-become-victims-of-genericization/)).

That means **nobody can stop you using the word.** But it also means:

**You almost certainly cannot register it as a trademark for this game.** Distinctiveness is
assessed relative to the goods. For a **board game whose subject matter is a laundromat**,
"LAUNDROMAT" is at best **merely descriptive** under 15 U.S.C. §1052(e)(1) — it describes a
feature/subject of the goods. Expected outcome: a descriptiveness refusal, with the fallback of
the **Supplemental Register** (which gives you very little) or a five-year acquired-distinctiveness
claim you will never have the sales to support. Filing costs **$350 per class** (the base USPTO
fee since the 18 Jan 2025 restructure eliminated TEAS Plus/Standard), plus surcharges of +$100 for
insufficient information and +$200 for custom goods descriptions; a clearance search and opinion
from counsel runs **$300–800**
([USPTO fee restructure](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/overview-of-key-uspto-trademark-filing-1143372),
[tmarkmetric 2026 fee schedule](https://tmarkmetric.com/insights/uspto-trademark-fees-2026)).

**So: unprotectable name, and you'd be paying $350–1,150 to be told so.**

### 6.2 The practical position is worse than the legal one

- **There is already a published board game called *Laundromat* in the BGG database**
  ([BGG #153641](https://boardgamegeek.com/boardgame/153641/laundromat)) — a card game about
  picking up women at laundromats. You would be the *second* "Laundromat" on BGG, forever
  disambiguated by year, and you'd inherit its search results.
- **There is also *Goblin Laundromat*** ([BGG #439804](https://boardgamegeek.com/boardgame/439804/goblin-laundromat)),
  plus *Washing Lines*, *Monster Laundry*, *Washing Machine Game*, *Wash & Dry Bingo*, and
  *Tokyo Coin Laundry* in the same thematic space.
- **Searchability is catastrophic.** "Laundromat board game" competes with millions of pages about
  actual laundromats, a 2019 Steven Soderbergh film called *The Laundromat*, and the "Russian
  Laundromat" money-laundering scandal. You cannot win that SEO fight and you cannot buy the
  domain or the handles.
- A publisher will rename it anyway.

### 6.3 Recommendation: rename. Concretely.

**Rename before you spend a penny on art.** The name is load-bearing for the logo, the box and
every URL and handle you'll claim.

Criteria for the replacement:
1. **Coined, arbitrary, or an unusual two-word compound** — registrable, ownable, searchable.
2. **Retains the laundry read at a glance** (put "laundromat" in the tagline, not the mark).
3. **Zero BGG collisions, .com available, handle available on IG/TikTok/Bluesky/Discord.**
4. **Says "tactical, everyone shares the machines," not "sabotage"** — see §7.
5. **Two syllables to four, easy to say aloud at a table, no ambiguous spelling.**

Directions worth exploring (check availability yourself before committing — I have not cleared
these):

- **Spin cycle / machine language:** *Spin Cycle*, *Full Load*, *Hot Wash*, *Rinse Cycle*,
  *Heavy Soil*, *Delicates* ⚠️ several of these are common phrases and may already be taken.
- **Coined compounds (strongest trademark position):** *Washaway*, *Sudsworth*, *Spindry*,
  *Launderama*, *Wringle*, *Tumbletown*.
- **Character-forward (best if Jimothy survives development):** *Jimothy's Laundromat* — a
  personal-name-plus-generic construction is far more registrable than the bare generic, keeps
  the theme completely legible, gives you a mascot for the box, and is instantly ungoogleable-in-a-
  good-way. **This is my leading suggestion**, contingent on Jimothy getting a real mechanic per
  your own R5.2.
- **Mechanic-forward:** *Sorted*, *Whites & Colours*, *Dark Load*.

Then: tagline **"a tactical card game set in a laundromat"**, use "laundromat" freely in
descriptions and metadata, and file the invented mark in **Class 28** for **$350** with an
ID-Manual-approved description.

## 7. The prior-art problem as a marketing problem

### 7.1 Verifying the legal claim — it is essentially correct

**Game mechanics are not copyrightable.** 17 U.S.C. §102(b) excludes "any idea, procedure,
process, system, method of operation" from copyright. The US Copyright Office states plainly that
"copyright does not protect the idea for a game, its name or title, or the method or methods for
playing it." Courts have applied this directly to card games — see the coverage of
*DaVinci Editrice v. Ziko Games* (S.D. Tex. 2016), holding that copyright did not protect the
structure and gameplay of a card game
([Lexology summary](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=05891d4f-1658-4f00-884f-8310cfeb4b0f);
[Copyright Alliance](https://copyrightalliance.org/copyrightability-of-board-games/);
[ABA Landslide, "Not Playing Around"](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/intellectual_property_law/resources/landslide/archive/not-playing-around-board-games-intellectual-property-law/)).

What **is** protected: **rulebook text as written**, **card illustrations**, **box art**, **graphic
design**, **custom sculpts**, **original characters and story** — and the **name** (trademark),
and distinctive **trade dress** (overall look and packaging).

### 7.2 So what is the genuine residual risk?

Small but non-zero. Four things to actually check and avoid:

1. **Don't copy rulebook language.** Write every word yourself. Do not paraphrase closely from
   *Dirty Laundry*'s rules. This is the most common way a "mechanics aren't copyrightable" defence
   gets complicated.
2. **Don't copy trade dress.** Different box shape, different palette, different typographic
   register, different art style, different logo lockup. If your box looks like theirs on a shelf,
   the fact that your rules are different won't help.
3. **Check for patents.** Game mechanics *can* be patented (rarely, and it's expensive), and design
   patents can cover component appearance. **Run a search on Quokka Games and the *Dirty Laundry*
   designers in Google Patents / Espacenet / IP Australia before launch** — it's free and takes an
   hour. I have not found any, but absence of a search result is not a clearance opinion. ⚠️
4. **Don't reuse their distinctive named elements.** If they have a named character, a named card,
   or a coined term, avoid it. Your "red sock ruins the whites" premise is real-world common
   knowledge and is fine; a coined card name of theirs would not be.

**If you rename away from a laundry-generic title and hire your own artist, your legal exposure
is effectively nil.** The problem is commercial, not legal.

### 7.3 The commercial problem, stated plainly

*Dirty Laundry: Sabotage Shedding Game* (Quokka Games, 2021,
[BGG](https://boardgamegeek.com/boardgame/353938/dirty-laundry-sabotage-shedding-game)) shares your
premise, your communal machines, your red-sock core, and your win condition. Every reviewer,
publisher and backer who looks at your game will find it in about eight seconds. **You do not get
to decide whether the comparison happens. You only get to decide whether you frame it or someone
else does.**

There is also a second-order risk your comparables doc doesn't name: *Dirty Laundry* is not a
well-known or well-regarded game. It has a small BGG footprint. So the comparison doesn't only
cost you novelty — it risks putting you in a **category that has already been tried and didn't
land**, which is a harder objection to answer than "someone got there first."

### 7.4 How to position against it — concretely

**1. Name the comparison first, in one line, in every pitch.**
Put it on the sell sheet. Publishers respect a designer who knows their category; they distrust one
who appears not to have looked. One line, no defensiveness:

> *"The nearest neighbour is Dirty Laundry (Quokka, 2021) — a hidden-information sabotage game.
> This is its inverse: everything is face-up, and the whole game is reading a shared board."*

**2. Lead with the mechanic, not the theme.** The laundry theme is the *hook*, not the *pitch*.
Your pitch is the open-information contested-space puzzle. Compare yourself to **6 nimmt!,
Coloretto and Kingdomino**, not to laundry games. This reframes the category from "laundry games"
(where you're second) to "open shared-space tactical games" (where you're novel *because* of the
theme). Your own comparables research already did this work — use it.

**3. Make the differentiator visible in a photograph.** This is the practical test. A backer
scrolling past your campaign image should see a **fully face-up table** — every machine's contents
visible, every hand of cards in the open. If your box back and your first campaign image show a
table where nothing is hidden, you've communicated the difference without saying a word. Design the
components toward this: big readable garment icons, machine tiles that display their contents,
no card backs facing anyone.

**4. Pick a tonal register they didn't take.** *Dirty Laundry* is "sabotage." Do not use the words
sabotage, ruin, revenge, or take-that anywhere in your marketing, even though your game has
interference in it. The register you want is **puzzle, timing, reading the table, cozy-but-sharp**.
The Trash Pandas / Coloretto shelf, not the Munchkin shelf.

**5. Differentiate on player count and length — honestly.** You're 3–6 players, 45–135 minutes.
That's a *different product* from a 20-minute filler. Say so. "A full-length tactical game, not a
filler" is a real positioning axis and it's one you own.

**6. Consider whether the theme is worth the collision at all.** Uncomfortable but necessary
question: your mechanic — communal shared spaces where dominant items spoil the rest, resolved by a
precedence ladder — does not *require* laundry. It requires a real-world "X ruins Y" intuition,
which is what makes the theme good. The laundry mapping is genuinely excellent (your comparables
doc is right about that) and I would keep it. But **if a publisher offers to license and re-theme,
the collision is an argument for saying yes, not for saying no.**

## Recommended path

For a designer with a heavily-playtested game, no art, no publisher, no audience, and — per the
comparables doc — five significant unresolved design risks.

**Headline: pitch it to publishers. Do not self-publish this game. Do not crowdfund it in 2026.**

Self-publishing this specific title would cost **$17,000–32,000** and 18–24 months, against a
product profile (3–6 players, 45–135 min, no solo mode, direct prior-art collision, generic
unregistrable name) that is unusually hard to sell as a first-time publisher, in a US import market
that is currently the worst it has been in a decade. The expected value is negative.

### Phase 0 — Fix the design and the name (0–3 months, ~$400)

| Step | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Resolve the OPEN items and the top-5 risks in `comparables.md` | $0 | Especially: the modal-null reckoning, the roll-4 attack, key fairness, tier-2 inversion, and Gang. A publisher will reject on these; better you fix them. |
| **Rename**; clear BGG, USPTO TESS, .com and social handles | $0 | See §6.3. Do this before art. |
| Cut the game's playtime ceiling | $0 | 135 minutes is a serious commercial handicap for a card game. If you can bring the ceiling to ~75 minutes you materially widen the publisher pool. |
| Build a **Screentop.gg** version | $0 | Playtest rig + demo + pitch asset. |
| Buy the **Cardboard Edison Compendium** | ~$5/mo | The publisher list. |
| Free patent search on Quokka Games | $0 | 1 hour, Google Patents + Espacenet. |
| 20 more blind playtests with strangers via Protospiel Online / BGDL | $0 | Blind tests, no teaching. |

### Phase 1 — Build the pitch package (3–5 months, $700–2,000)

| Step | Cost |
|---|---|
| Write a clean, edited **rulebook PDF** (hire a rules editor) | $300–900 |
| **One-page sell sheet** — hook, specs, 3 bullets, the prior-art line, contact | $0–250 (a designer can make this look right for $250) |
| **4-minute how-to-play video** over the Screentop build | $0 |
| **Two POD prototypes** from The Game Crafter, presentable but not pretty | $50–90 |
| Enough placeholder graphic design that the prototype reads clearly | $0–500 |
| **Do not commission illustration.** | $0 |

That last line is the important one. Publishers explicitly do not want finished art — they have
their own artists and their own look. Art is the single biggest cost in this whole document and
the licensing path lets you skip it entirely.

### Phase 2 — Pitch (6–18 months, $500–2,500)

| Step | Cost |
|---|---|
| Email 30–50 publishers from the Compendium, targeted, in waves of 10 | $0 |
| Attend **one** convention as an attendee with pre-booked publisher meetings — UKGE (June), Gen Con (Aug), Essen (Oct), PAX Unplugged (Dec) | $500–2,500 incl. travel |
| Enter **Cardboard Edison Award** and similar design contests | $20–60 each |
| Enter Gen Con **First Exposure Playtest Hall** / UKGE playtest zone | badge cost |
| Post design diaries on your BGG blog throughout | $0 |

Realistic outcome: **30–60 pitches, 2–8 requests for a prototype, 0–2 offers.** That is a normal,
healthy ratio, not a failure. Terms if signed: **5–8% of wholesale, $500–2,000 advance, 2.5–4 years
to shelf** (§3.1). At a 2,000-copy first print run of a $30 game, that's ~$1,400 gross to you.
**Take it anyway** — the credential is worth more than the cheque, and it costs you nothing.

### Phase 3a — If someone signs it

Do the developer's notes, cash the advance, and **start your second game immediately.** Designers
who make a living do it on a catalogue of 5–15 licensed titles, not one.

### Phase 3b — If nobody signs it after ~18 months

Two honest options.

**Option A (recommended): a deliberate small edition, treated as a portfolio piece, not a business.**

| Step | Cost |
|---|---|
| One artist doing illustration + graphic design, stylized flat vector, ~26 pieces | $3,500–5,500 |
| Rules editing, layout | included / $300 |
| Short-run offset, **500 units**, recommended spec (punchboard, standee raccoon, no custom plastics) | ~$2,400 ex-works |
| Freight + duty (US ⚠️ / UK domestic) | $800–2,000 / $300–600 |
| 30 review copies + postage | $600–1,100 |
| Modest ad spend + BGG/Kickstarter presence | $1,500–3,000 |
| **Total** | **$9,000–14,000 (US) / $8,000–11,000 (UK/EU)** |

Fund it on **Kickstarter** with a public goal of **$9,000–12,000** and an honest internal
requirement of ~$18,000. Spend **6 months** first building to **2,000+ KS followers and 1,500+
emails** (§4.3) — through BGG design diaries, the Screentop demo, short-form video of the table,
and the design-community channels. If you cannot reach ~1,500 followers in six months, **do not
launch** — that is the signal, and heeding it saves you $15,000.

**Option B: leave it on The Game Crafter and move on.** Zero risk, a permanent BGG entry, a real
credential, 70–85% of the profit on whatever trickles out, and your energy freed for game two.
This is a more respectable outcome than most first-time designers admit.

### Timeline and money, summarized

| Path | Cash at risk | Time to a physical product | Realistic upside |
|---|---|---|---|
| **License (recommended)** | **$1,200–5,000** | 2.5–4 years, publisher-controlled | $1,400–7,200 royalties per print run; a credential; artistic input but not control |
| Self-publish, 500 units | $9,000–14,000 | 12–18 months | Break-even at ~450 sold; $3,000–8,000 profit if it sells out |
| Self-publish, 1,000+ units via Kickstarter | $17,000–32,000 | 18–24 months | $10,000–40,000 raise; the modal outcome for a first-timer with no list is a failed campaign |
| POD only | ~$0–500 | 2 months | ~$5–8/copy on a handful of sales |

### The three things that matter most, in order

1. **Rename the game.** Free, and it unblocks everything downstream.
2. **Fix the five design risks in `comparables.md` before pitching.** Free, and it's what a
   publisher will actually judge you on.
3. **Do not buy art until someone else is paying for it, or until you have 1,500 people who want
   the game.** Art is $5,000–13,000 and it is the money most first-time designers lose.
